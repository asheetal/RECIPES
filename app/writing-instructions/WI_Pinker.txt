COMPREHENSIVE SUMMARY OF KEY PRINCIPLES FROM:
Steven Pinker, The Sense of Style: The Thinking Person's Guide to Writing in the 21st Century (2014)

This document summarizes all major principles from all six chapters. It is intended as a reference
to guide writing that follows Pinker's standards. Principles are actionable and specific.

=========================================================
CHAPTER 1: GOOD WRITING — DEVELOPING A WRITERLY EAR
=========================================================

The path to good writing begins with reading good prose closely and analytically. Writers acquire
technique by spotting, savoring, and reverse-engineering examples of excellent writing. Style cannot
be taught by commandments alone; it must be absorbed from models.

Good writing shares these properties across diverse styles and authors:
- Fresh wording and concrete imagery over familiar verbiage and abstract summary.
- Attention to the reader's vantage point: the writer controls what the reader sees and when.
- The judicious placement of an uncommon or surprising word against a backdrop of simple nouns
  and verbs. A varied vocabulary and the use of unusual words distinguish sprightly prose from mush.
  The best words not only pinpoint an idea precisely but echo it in sound (phonesthetics).
- Structural parallelism: parallel syntax is a powerful device for elegance and clarity.
- Occasional planned surprise — a stark revelation, an abrupt ending, a zeugma.
- Telling detail that allows the reader to draw a conclusion without the author stating it explicitly.
- Meter and sound that resonate with the meaning and mood.
- Writers do not hide passion or relish. They write as if they have something important to show.

Good writing starts strong — not with a cliché or a banality, but with a contentful observation that
provokes curiosity. It finishes strong as well.

Good writing shows; it does not tell. Rather than asserting that something is moving or important, it
presents a specific image or detail that generates the feeling in the reader.

Prose is brought to life with concrete images, similes, metaphors, and well-chosen idioms. Fiction
and nonfiction share many of the same expository techniques.

CHAPTER 2: CLASSIC STYLE — THE GUIDING METAPHOR
=========================================================

THE CLASSIC STYLE IDEAL

The governing metaphor of classic style is seeing the world. The writer has noticed something true
and orients the reader's gaze so she can see it for herself. Prose is a transparent window onto ideas,
not a performance of the writer's erudition, caution, or anxiety.

Properties of classic style:
- The writer's purpose is presentation; the motive is disinterested truth.
- The truth can be known and is not the same as the language that reveals it.
- The writer and reader are equals engaged in a conversation.
- The writer credits the reader with intelligence: she can recognize the truth when she sees it.
- Classic style is confident, not arrogant. It is direct without being dismissive of complexity.
- Classic prose is a pretense — the writer has worked hard to make the result seem effortless.

Classic style differs from:
- Practical style: memos, manuals, term papers written to satisfy defined roles.
- Romantic or contemplative style: sharing ineffable personal reactions.
- Oracular or prophetic style: inspiring a crowd with the music of language.
- Self-conscious, relativistic, postmodern style: foregrounding anxiety about the act of writing,
  hedging every claim, and refusing to commit to a viewpoint.

Classic style is not the same as avoiding abstraction. It can explain abstract ideas — even quantum
cosmology — by treating them as objects and forces a reader could recognize if positioned to see them.
Brian Greene's explanation of the multiverse (cited by Pinker) exemplifies this: he narrates a series
of events and trusts that any careful reader who can see them will know what they imply.

METADISCOURSE

Metadiscourse is writing about the writing rather than about the subject matter. It bogs down prose
without conveying content. Eliminate it.

Examples to avoid:
- "In this paper, we will argue that..."
- "Having established X, we now turn to Y."
- "This section reviews the literature on..."
- "As noted above..." / "As we will see below..."
- "It is important to note that..." / "It is worth noting that..."
- "In summary..." / "To recap..."
- "This section discusses the factors that..."

Readers do not need narration of what is happening; they need the content itself. Trust the structure
of the prose to guide the reader.

A limited amount of signposting is acceptable in conversation and in writing, but it should be done
sparingly, as naturally as one would in conversation. Good alternatives to metadiscourse:
- Open with a question ("What makes a name rise in popularity?") instead of announcing the topic.
- Use the language of vision: "As we have seen..." puts the writer and reader in the scene together.
- Use the language of movement: "Now that we have explored X, we arrive at the puzzle of Y."

PROFESSIONAL NARCISSISM

Many writers confuse the world of their subject matter with the world of their profession. The result
is the typical academic opening that describes researchers' activities rather than the subject they study:
"In recent years, increasing numbers of psychologists have turned their attention to..."
Classic style ignores the hired help and looks directly at what they are paid to study:
"All children acquire a language without explicit lessons. How?"

HEDGING

Writers over-hedge from fear of being wrong. Excessive hedging makes prose tedious and signals
that the writer does not believe their own claims.

Avoid: somewhat, rather, fairly, relatively, arguably, it could be said that, in some sense, to some
extent, it may be the case that, perhaps, might (overused), I would argue, appear to, seem to.

Where uncertainty is real and relevant, state it once and specifically — spell out the actual conditions
under which the claim does not hold — then move on. Do not hedge every clause.

Intensifiers such as very, highly, and extremely also function as hedges: they replace categorical
affirmation with a graduated scale. "He is an honest man" is more reassuring than "He is a very
honest man." Intensifiers are usually deletable.

SCARE QUOTES

Do not use quotation marks to distance yourself from a word or phrase you have chosen to use.
Quotation marks signal that the writer is not using a word in its standard meaning, or is attributing it
to someone else. Using them apologetically ("he tried to 'think outside the box'") implies you could
not find a better phrase, that you do not believe your own word, or that you seek immunity from the
idiom without giving it up. If you are not comfortable using an expression without quotation marks,
do not use it.

CLICHÉS AND MIXED METAPHORS

A cliché-monger turns off their own visual brain and plants dead idioms on the page. The reader
stops converting language into mental images and merely mouths the words. Worse, mixed metaphors
produce ludicrous imagery.

Good writers:
- Reach for fresh similes and metaphors that engage the reader's sensory cortex.
- When a shopworn idiom is unavoidable, remember what it literally means and play with the image
  to revive it in the reader's mind.
- Use phonesthetically appropriate words: words whose sound echoes their meaning.

ABSTRACT NOUNS (NOMINALIZATION)

Nominalization converts verbs and adjectives into abstract nouns by adding suffixes such as –ance,
–ment, –ation, or –ing. The writing scholar Helen Sword calls them "zombie nouns" because they
lumber across the page without a conscious agent directing them.

Avoid: conceptualization, utilization, implementation, the finding, the assumption, investigation,
examination, consideration, facilitation, the occurrence of, anticipation, cancellation, the existence
of, a tendency to, the fact that.

Prefer the verb or adjective:
Bad:  "The conceptualization of the phenomenon resulted in an increase in understanding."
Better: "Conceptualizing the phenomenon helped us understand it."
Better still: "We understood the phenomenon better once we conceptualized it."

Also watch for metaconcepts — words that serve as packing material around the real content:
level, issue, context, framework, approach, model, perspective, variable, process, range, strategy,
condition, tendency. Ask whether the phrase "on the aspirational level" adds anything that "to aspire"
does not. It does not.

PASSIVE VOICE

Passive voice is appropriate and sometimes indispensable, but it is widely misused.

Passive is appropriate when:
(a) The agent is unknown or irrelevant ("The honey was eaten").
(b) The patient of the action is the topic of the discourse and should appear first for coherence.
(c) The agent is minor or distracting ("Helicopters were flown in to put out the fires").
(d) The passive shortens the gap between a relative-clause filler and its gap, easing comprehension.

Passive is problematic when:
- It hides the agent to evade responsibility ("mistakes were made").
- It is used out of habit rather than purpose, leaving the reader without an agent to visualize.
- It displaces the reader's spotlight from the character doing the acting.

Use the active voice whenever the agent is the natural focus of the reader's attention. Active
constructions train the reader's gaze on who is doing something; passive constructions train the
reader's gaze on what is being done to whom.

FIRST- AND SECOND-PERSON PRONOUNS

The pronouns I, we, and you simulate a conversation, as classic style recommends. Using them
reduces the reader's memory load and is more natural than bureaucratic evasion of the first person.
The social psychologist Gordon Allport summarized the problem: "Please, sir; I didn't do it! It was
done!" Use I when you mean I.

CHAPTER 3: THE CURSE OF KNOWLEDGE
=========================================================

THE CENTRAL PROBLEM

The single most important explanation for bad writing is the "curse of knowledge": writers know
what they mean, so they forget that readers do not. They omit the context, definitions, and concrete
examples that would make their meaning clear to a reader who lacks their background.

The curse of knowledge is not malice or pretension; it is a failure of imagination. Bright, honest
writers fall into it because knowing something well prevents them from noticing that others do not
know it.

Related cognitive phenomena:
- Egocentrism: inability to imagine a scene from another's vantage point.
- Hindsight bias: what we know now seems like it should have been obvious before.
- False consensus: assuming others would make the same decisions we did.
- Illusory transparency: assuming others can detect what we know to be the case.
- The better you know something, the less you remember how hard it was to learn.

JARGON AND ABBREVIATIONS

Every specialty develops argot. The curse of knowledge causes writers to overestimate how standard
a term has become. Guidelines:
- Define or gloss all technical terms with a brief phrase in plain language.
- Spell out abbreviations on first use. Never coin an abbreviation for a single paper.
- Use examples liberally. An explanation without an example is little better than no explanation.
- When technical terms are unavoidable, prefer those with transparent, memorable labels.

CHUNKING AND FUNCTIONAL FIXITY

As experts learn, they bundle related concepts into single mental units (chunks) and begin to think of
objects in terms of their function rather than their physical form (functional fixity). The result:
writers refer to what they do rather than what they observe, and readers, who have not made the
same journey through abstraction, cannot follow.

The cure is to unpack the chunk and narrate the concrete events or objects that compose it:
"Social avoidance" → the mouse cowering in the corner of a cage that contains another mouse.
"Participants read assertions whose veracity was affirmed" → We presented a sentence, followed by
the word TRUE or FALSE.

HOW TO LIFT THE CURSE

1. Mentally simulate the reader's perspective at every step. Assume they know considerably less than
   you think they do.
2. Show a draft to actual readers who are similar to the intended audience. Revise in response to
   comments that recur across readers or that make sense to you when you re-read.
3. Revisit a draft after enough time has passed that the text is no longer familiar.
4. Polish draft after draft. Good writing is produced through multiple rounds of revision.

The form in which thoughts occur to a writer is rarely the same as the form in which they can be
absorbed by a reader. The advice in this and other stylebooks is not so much on how to write as on
how to revise.

CHAPTER 4: THE WEB, THE TREE, AND THE STRING — SYNTAX
=========================================================

MENTAL MODELS OF SYNTAX

A sentence converts a web of ideas in the writer's mind into a linear string of words. The conversion
is accomplished by a tree of phrases. Each phrase is embedded inside larger phrases according to
rules of English syntax (grammar).

Understanding the treelike structure of sentences helps a writer:
- Avoid grammatical errors (agreement, case, coordination).
- Parse ambiguities and garden paths before they reach the reader.
- Arrange words so the reader can absorb them in the right order with minimum cognitive effort.

AGREEMENT

Subject-verb agreement is defined over trees, not strings. When a bushy phrase separates a subject
from its verb, the verb must agree with the head of the subject phrase, not with the most recently
heard noun:
Wrong: "The readiness of our conventional forces are at an all-time low."
Right:  "The readiness of our conventional forces is at an all-time low."

To avoid agreement errors: mentally strip the subject phrase down to its head noun, then check
whether the verb agrees with that noun alone.

COORDINATION

Each phrase in a coordination (joined by and, or, but) must work independently in its position.
Miscoordinated phrases — where the elements joined by not only…but also, both…and,
either…or, neither…nor are grammatically mismatched — grate on careful readers. The cure is to
ensure the phrases following each marker are parallel in grammatical form.

CASE

Pronoun case (nominative vs. accusative) must follow the position the pronoun occupies in the tree,
not its proximity to other words. The most common error is hypercorrection: using nominative
"I" or "he" after a coordinator because writers have been corrected for using "me" at the start.
Check case by removing the coordination and testing the pronoun alone:
"Give Al Gore and me a chance" (not "and I") — confirmed by "Give me a chance."

WHO AND WHOM

Who is nominative (replaces subjects: he, she, they). Whom is accusative (replaces objects: him,
her, them). To verify the right choice, mentally move the wh-word back to its original position in
the sentence and substitute he/him or she/her. If him or her fits, use whom; if he or she fits, use who.
In casual prose, who is acceptable in many positions where traditionalists would insist on whom.

SENTENCE GEOMETRY: LEFT-BRANCHING, RIGHT-BRANCHING, CENTER-EMBEDDED

Right-branching sentences (in which the most complicated phrase comes last) are the easiest to
parse. English is predominantly right-branching.

Left-branching sentences (in which a long modifier precedes the main clause or noun) make the
reader parse many words before learning what the sentence is about. Keep left branches short.

Center-embedded sentences (in which a clause is jammed into the middle of a larger clause) are the
hardest to parse and often produce the sensation of "convoluted" or "tortuous" syntax. Disinter the
embedded clauses and place them side by side, or break the sentence into two or more sentences.

OMITTING NEEDLESS WORDS

Every word added to a sentence imposes two cognitive demands on the reader: understanding the
word and fitting it into the tree. Many common phrases are morbidly obese:
- "make an appearance" → appear
- "is capable of being" → can be
- "in the event that" → if
- "in view of the fact that" → since
- "owing to the fact that" → because
- "have a facilitative impact" → help
- "It is widely observed that X" → X
- "at such time as" → when
- "for the purpose of" → to
- "in the matter of" → about

Light verbs (make, do, have, bring, put, take) often exist only to create a slot for a zombie noun.
Replace make an appearance with appear; put on a performance with perform.

Sentences beginning with "It is" or "There is" are often candidates for compression:
"There is competition between groups for resources" → "Groups compete for resources."

Brevity is not the goal per se. Good writers use long sentences when they are right-branching and
the reader can absorb the content phrase by phrase. The goal is to eliminate words that impose cost
without conveying content.

PARSING AIDS: AVOIDING GARDEN PATHS AND AMBIGUITY

A garden path is a sentence that lures the reader into a misparse, forcing her to backtrack.

Causes of garden paths:
- Statistically frequent word pairs that belong to different phrases ("The data point to...")
- Ambiguous words whose less frequent sense is the intended one.
- Phrases that naturally attach to the nearest eligible host, not to the intended one.

Remedies:
1. Read the sentence aloud. If you stumble, the reader will too.
2. Punctuate properly. Commas mark supplementary phrases; their absence marks integrated ones.
3. Retain signal words: the subordinator "that," relative pronouns "which" and "who," and the
   definite article "the" guide the reader into the right phrase and prevent wrong-turn attachments.
4. Use structural parallelism. A syntactic template lingering from a previous phrase helps the reader
   slot new material into the right structure.
5. Move phrases so that related words stay connected in the tree (not necessarily in the string).
   Pull apart phrase pairs that attract each other but belong to different parts of the sentence.

WORD ORDER PRINCIPLES

English word order must reconcile two competing demands:
(a) Syntactic demand: English requires subject before verb before object.
(b) Cognitive demands: (i) light before heavy, (ii) given before new (topic before comment).

Techniques to reconcile these demands:
- The passive voice: allows a doer to appear late in the sentence when it is heavy, new, or both.
- Preposing: move a modifier leftward to tie the sentence to what came before.
- Postposing: shift a heavy phrase rightward so it arrives when the reader's other work is done.
- Existential construction ("There was a shepherd..."): delays heavy or new information.
- Cleft ("It was Oedipus who killed Laius"): focuses attention on a specific element.
- Pseudo-cleft ("What Oedipus did was kill Laius"): similar focusing device.
- Choice of verb: verbs like rob/steal, buy/sell, give/receive encode the same event with different
  roles in subject vs. object position.

Begin a sentence with familiar or given information; place new and heavy information toward the end.

CHAPTER 5: ARCS OF COHERENCE
=========================================================

WHAT COHERENCE REQUIRES

Even if every sentence in a passage is individually clear, a succession of them can feel choppy,
disjointed, and incoherent. Coherence requires three things working together:
(a) A consistent topic (what the passage is about).
(b) A clear point (what the writer is trying to accomplish by exploring the topic).
(c) Visible logical connections between one sentence and the next.

Reveal the topic and the point near the beginning of any section or essay. A reader who does not
know the topic cannot understand the prose, no matter how explicit the language. As newspaper
editors say: do not bury the lede.

TOPIC STRINGS

The topic of a discourse corresponds to the subject matter into which incoming sentences should be
merged. The topic of a sentence is typically its grammatical subject. In coherent prose, these align:
the subject of each sentence corresponds to what the passage is about.

Practical rules:
- Keep the protagonist, agent, or main entity in the subject position throughout a passage.
- Shift topic when necessary, but announce the shift and maintain it consistently.
- Use the passive voice strategically to keep a topic in subject position even when it is not the agent
  of the action ("One hungry heron was seen chowing down a litter of feral kittens").
- Prepose temporal and other modifiers to vary sentence rhythm without displacing the topic.

GIVEN BEFORE NEW

New information should appear toward the end of the sentence, after the familiar or given
information that anchors it. Do not hurl a new fact at the reader before she has context for it.

THE DEFINITE AND INDEFINITE ARTICLES

Articles signal whether an entity is being introduced for the first time (a/an) or is already known to
the reader (the). Introduce a character or entity with an indefinite noun phrase; refer to it
subsequently with a definite noun phrase or pronoun.

On subsequent appearances, choose between pronoun, definite noun, and varied label as follows:
- A pronoun signals "you already know this person; no need to stop and consider them anew."
- Repeat the same definite noun phrase when the pronoun is ambiguous or too much time has passed.
- A varied label (a more generic term) can serve as an honorary pronoun when repeating the name
  would sound monotonous or would misleadingly suggest a new individual has entered the scene.
  The substitute must be more generic than the original (a "bus" → "the vehicle") and must
  immediately call the original to mind.
- Do not use varied labels capriciously. Elegant variation — the "fear of using the same word twice"
  (synonymomania) — confuses readers who assume different words refer to different things.
- Vary wording only when an entity appears multiple times in quick succession and repetition would
  sound monotonous or misleadingly suggest a new actor.

When comparing or contrasting two things, never vary the wording. Use parallel syntax and vary
only the word that captures the actual difference. Varying syntax while comparing is like changing
two variables in a single experiment: the reader cannot determine which variation caused what.

ZOMBIE NOUNS IN COHERENT DISCOURSE

Nominalizations are not always wrong. On second mention, a nominalized event (the cancellation,
the refusal) serves as a pronoun for that event, allowing reference without tedious repetition.
The problem with zombie nouns arises on first mention: the writer has already been thinking about
the event and treats it as old news, while the reader is encountering it for the first time and needs
to see it enacted in concrete language before it can be summarized.

COHERENCE RELATIONS

Every sentence must connect logically to the one before it. Readers are so eager for coherence that
they will infer a connection even when none was intended — and will sometimes infer the wrong one.

The major families of coherence relations (with typical connectives):

RESEMBLANCE RELATIONS
- Similarity: "and," "similarly," "likewise" — Two propositions similar except in one respect; the
  connective calls attention to what they share.
- Contrast: "but," "in contrast," "on the other hand," "alternatively," "whereas" — Two
  propositions similar except in one respect; the connective calls attention to the difference.
- Elaboration: colon, "that is," "in other words," "furthermore," "also" — General statement
  followed by specific detail.
- Exemplification: "for example," "for instance," "such as," "including" — Generalization followed
  by example(s).
- Generalization: "in general," "more generally" — Example(s) followed by generalization.
- Exception: "however," "nonetheless," "nevertheless," "then there is" — A generalization and a
  case that violates it.

CONTIGUITY RELATIONS (SEQUENCE)
- Before-and-after: "and," "before," "then."
- After-and-before: "after," "once," "when."
All things being equal, describe events in chronological order: easier to process.
When the spotlight has been on a later event and an earlier one must be introduced, the
imperative to mention given before new overrides the chronological preference.

CAUSAL RELATIONS
- Result (cause-effect): "and," "as a result," "therefore," "so."
- Explanation (effect-cause): "because," "since."
- Violated expectation (preventer-effect): "but," "however," "nonetheless," "yet."
- Failed prevention (effect-preventer): "despite," "even though."

ATTRIBUTION
- "according to," "stated that," "argues that" — Used to distinguish the writer's own claims from
  those of others. Attribution must be unambiguous.

Key guidance on connectives:
- Too many connectives make prose pedantic; too few leave the reader puzzled.
- Calibrate the number to the reader's expertise. Expert readers already know many connections;
  spelling out obvious ones wastes their time and may confuse them.
- Never use two connectives to signal the same connection ("the reason is because" — reason
  already implies a causal explanation).
- Because of the curse of knowledge, writers more often err on the side of too few connectives
  than too many. When in doubt, connect.

NEGATION

Negative statements require the reader to form an affirmative proposition and then tag it "false" — 
extra cognitive work. Guidelines:
- Prefer affirmative statements where possible.
- Use negation when denying a proposition the reader is likely to entertain. Set up the plausible
  belief before denying it: "You might think X... but in fact Y."
- Do not negate a proposition the reader was not already considering; if you must, introduce it as
  plausible first.
- Avoid stacking negations (words like no, not, few, little, seldom, rarely, though, deny, refute,
  avoid, instead all carry negation). Multiple negations exhaust the reader and risk misnegation
  (inadvertently asserting the opposite of what you mean).
- Clarify the scope of negation: place "not" as close as possible to the phrase it negates.
  "We choose to go to the moon not because it is easy but because it is hard" — the position of
  "not" makes the scope unambiguous.

SENSE OF PROPORTION

Allocate verbiage in proportion to the centrality of each point to the argument. If 90 percent of
the evidence supports your position, devote roughly 90 percent of the discussion to it. If extended
counterarguments are necessary, give them their own section with a clearly stated purpose, rather
than allowing them to crowd the main argument and mislead the reader about the author's conclusion.

THEMATIC CONSISTENCY

A coherent text refers to each of its main themes in a consistent, transparent way throughout. The
terms used to describe a theme must be obviously related to each other so the reader can track the
theme without effort. Do not slip between loosely associated concepts without making their
connection explicit.

PARAGRAPHS AND STRUCTURE

There is no fixed unit of discourse that must correspond to a paragraph. What exists is the paragraph
break: a visual bookmark that lets the reader pause, assimilate, and find his place. Paragraph breaks
should coincide with divisions in the discourse tree.

Academic writers use too few paragraph breaks; journalists sometimes use too many. Show mercy to
your readers and let their eyes rest. Do not break a paragraph in the middle of a train of thought.

A coherent text is an engineered object: an ordered hierarchy of sections, paragraphs, and sentences,
crisscrossed by arcs that track topics, points, actors, and themes, and held together by connectives.

CHAPTER 6: TELLING RIGHT FROM WRONG — USAGE, GRAMMAR, AND PUNCTUATION
=========================================================

THE PRESCRIPTIVE/DESCRIPTIVE DISTINCTION

Rules of usage are tacit conventions, not logical truths or legislative decrees. No authority governs
English; usage emerges from an implicit consensus among writers, readers, and editors.

There are two kinds of rules:
(a) Descriptive rules: regularities in how all fluent speakers use the language.
(b) Prescriptive rules: conventions adopted by a community of literate writers for formal prose.

The goal of prescriptive rules is not to enforce logic but to signal care, reduce misunderstanding,
and ensure grammatical consistency that helps readers parse text. Many prescriptive rules are worth
following; many others are "bubbe meises" — folklore, not grammar — invented by self-proclaimed
authorities and refuted by both history and usage.

HOW TO DISTINGUISH LEGITIMATE RULES FROM FOLKLORE

Follow a rule if:
- Careful writers who flout it agree, when it is pointed out, that something has gone wrong.
- The best writers have respected it historically.
- It encodes a genuine semantic distinction.

Discard a rule if:
- It is based on a crackpot theory (e.g., English should imitate Latin).
- It is instantly refuted by the facts of English.
- It originated as the pet peeve of a self-anointed authority.
- Great writers have routinely flouted it.
- Applying it makes prose clumsier.
- It confuses a formal-style preference with a grammatical error.

When in doubt: look it up in a reputable modern usage guide or dictionary (American Heritage
Dictionary, Merriam-Webster Unabridged, Merriam-Webster's Dictionary of English Usage).

LEGITIMATE GRAMMATICAL RULES

Subject-verb agreement: The verb must agree with the head noun of the subject phrase, not the
most recently heard noun. Mental stripping of the subject to its head noun prevents this error.

Coordination: Each coordinate must be grammatically parallel and must function independently in
its position. Quantifier pairs (not only…but also, both…and, either…or, neither…nor) require the
bracketed material after each marker to match in grammatical form.

Case: Use nominative pronouns (I, he, she, we, they, who) as subjects of tensed verbs. Use
accusative pronouns (me, him, her, us, them, whom) as objects of verbs and prepositions.
Hypercorrection ("between you and I") is common but avoidable with tree-thinking.

Dangling modifiers: A misplaced modifier can lure a reader to an unintended or comical
interpretation. The remedy is not to treat danglers as ungrammatical per se but to revise when a
reader could plausibly attach the modifier to the wrong element. Many participial phrases that appear
to "dangle" are perfectly acceptable (especially when the subject is the writer and reader, or when
the participial phrase has become a preposition).

Sequence of tenses: In indirect discourse, the tense of an embedded verb is typically "backshifted"
to match the past tense of the verb of saying. Backshifting is not mandatory when the reported
content is a general or ongoing truth.

Split infinitives and "split verbs": The prohibition is a superstition with no basis in English
grammar. Unsplitting an infinitive often changes the meaning or forces an adverb into a less natural
position. Split infinitives when doing so places the adverb where it most naturally belongs.
("Are you sure you want to permanently delete all items?" is correct as written.)

LEGITIMATE SEMANTIC DISTINCTIONS (WORDS WORTH DISTINGUISHING)

The following distinctions matter and are worth preserving:
- disinterested (impartial, without a vested interest) vs. uninterested (bored)
- enormity (extreme evil or moral transgression) vs. enormousness (large size)
- refute (prove false) vs. deny or allege to be false
- flaunt (show off) vs. flout (disregard, violate)
- bemused (bewildered) vs. amused
- reticent (shy, restrained) vs. reluctant
- fortuitous (coincidental, accidental) vs. fortunate
- simplistic (naively oversimplified) vs. simple
- fulsome (excessively ingratiating) vs. full or copious
- criteria (plural) vs. criterion (singular); phenomena (plural) vs. phenomenon (singular)
- beg the question (circular reasoning) vs. raise the question
- literally (in actual fact) vs. figuratively (the common but confusing hyperbolic misuse)
- mitigate (alleviate) vs. militate (provide reasons against)

GRAMMATICAL FOLKLORE TO DISCARD

The following "rules" are not rules. Great writers have routinely ignored them for centuries:
- Never begin a sentence with a conjunction (and, but, or, because, so): false.
- Never end a sentence with a preposition: false.
- Never split an infinitive: false.
- "Which" may not introduce a restrictive relative clause: false. Both "that" and "which" are
  acceptable in restrictive relatives; the real decision is whether to use a restrictive or
  nonrestrictive clause, and that is marked by punctuation, not by the choice of which vs. that.
- Singular "they" is a grammatical error: false. "Singular they" (used with universally or
  negatively quantified antecedents) has been standard since Chaucer and is used by Austen,
  Shakespeare, and Shaw. It is the most natural solution to the problem of generic reference.
- "None" must be singular: false. None can be singular or plural depending on whether the writer
  is thinking of the group collectively or the members individually.
- "Between" applies only to two items: false. Between is appropriate whenever individuals are
  considered two at a time, regardless of the total count.

PUNCTUATION — MAJOR PRINCIPLES

The main purpose of punctuation is to resolve ambiguities and garden paths that would mislead a
reader if print consisted only of letters and spaces. Punctuation indicates prosody (pausing, stress)
and syntax (phrase boundaries), though neither consistently.

Commas have two main functions:
(a) Setting off supplementary (nonrestrictive, parenthetical) material from the core of the sentence.
    A supplementary phrase is one that adds a comment but is not essential to the sentence's meaning.
    Set it off with commas on both sides: "The senator, who chairs the committee, arrived late."
(b) Separating items in a list.

A comma may NOT separate integrated (restrictive) phrases — subject from predicate, verb from
complement, or a noun from an integrated relative clause — even when the writer would pause in
speech at that point.

Comma splice: Using a comma to join two complete sentences is an error. Two sentences may be
legitimately joined with a semicolon (all-purpose connector), a colon (for elaboration/exemplification),
a dash (for interruption or emphasis), a coordinating conjunction with a comma (and, but, or, so,
yet), or a subordinating conjunction (because, although, when, etc.).

Serial (Oxford) comma: Use it. Omitting the final comma before "and" in a list can create
ambiguity or garden paths. Use semicolons to separate items in lists that themselves contain commas.

Apostrophe rules:
- Plurals do NOT take apostrophes: "apples," not "apple's."
- Contractions DO take apostrophes: you're (you are), it's (it is), he's (he is).
- The genitive of singular nouns takes 's: "Charles's son," "Sophocles' play" (ancient names).
- The genitive of plural nouns takes s': "parents' house."
- Possessive pronouns (its, your, their, whose, hers, ours) take NO apostrophe.

Quotation marks: Use them to reproduce someone else's words, to mention a word as a word, or to
signal that a word is being used in a sense the writer does not endorse. Do NOT use them for
emphasis.

ADDITIONAL PRINCIPLES APPLICABLE ACROSS ALL WRITING
=========================================================

"WHILE" AND "WHERE" (GRAMMATICAL PRECISION)

While = during the same time. Use only when two events or states are simultaneous.
Correct:   "Participants completed the survey while the experimenter left the room."
Incorrect: "Study 1 examined persistence, while Study 2 examined motivation." (Use "whereas.")

Where = in a place or location. Use only when a location is literally meant.
Correct:   "We recruited participants from a community where income inequality was high."
Incorrect: "We adopted a design where counterbalancing was used." (Use "in which.")

AVOID REPETITION AND SUMMARY

Good writing does not repeat what it has already said. Summarizing prior points insults the reader's
intelligence and memory. Each sentence should advance the argument. Readers are not stupid and do
not have bad memories.

Avoid:
- Opening a paragraph by restating the prior paragraph's conclusion.
- Closing a section by summarizing what the section just covered.
- Transition sentences that merely announce what the reader is about to read.
- Any sentence that restates an earlier one in different words without adding information.

WORD CHOICE: PRECISION AND DIRECTNESS

Choose the word that says exactly what you mean, not the word that sounds most impressive. Prefer
shorter, Anglo-Saxon words over longer Latinate synonyms when the shorter word is equally precise:
"use" over "utilize," "start" over "commence," "end" over "terminate."

Do not use a word to signal membership in a scholarly community; use it because it conveys a
distinction that a simpler word would not.

Words and phrases to avoid (overused, imprecise, or evasive):
- critical, crucial, profound, fundamental (overused intensifiers)
- interesting, important, significant (assert why; do not merely claim it)
- clearly, obviously (if it were obvious, you would not need to say it)
- very, quite, rather (usually deletable)
- in terms of (almost always replaceable by a single preposition: about, for, in, on)
- the fact that (usually deletable; restructure the sentence)
- it is worth noting that (delete the frame; just note it)
- as such (frequently misused to mean "therefore"; it means "in that capacity")
- as of this writing (usually redundant; omit)
- needless to say (if needless to say, do not say it)

INTELLECTUAL STANDARDS UNDERLYING GOOD WRITING

Good style and intellectual conscientiousness are linked. Writers should:
1. Look things up. Do not rely on memory or conventional wisdom; verify claims.
2. Ensure arguments are sound: conclusions must follow from premises reasonable people accept.
3. Do not confuse an anecdote or a single observation with a trend or a general truth.
4. Avoid false dichotomies. Complex issues rarely reduce to a battle between two slogans.
5. Evaluate arguments on their merits, not on the character or motives of the people making them.

As the linguist Ann Farmer observed: "It isn't about being right. It's about getting it right."

The reason to care about style is ultimately not cosmetic. Good style enhances the spread of ideas,
exemplifies attention to detail, and adds to the beauty of the world.
